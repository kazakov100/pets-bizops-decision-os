Michael E. Porter's Five Forces framework (introduced in "How Competitive
Forces Shape Strategy," Harvard Business Review, 1979; revisited in "The Five
Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy," HBR 2008) explains the long-run
profitability of an industry through five structural forces: (1) threat of new
entrants, (2) bargaining power of suppliers, (3) bargaining power of buyers,
(4) threat of substitute products or services, and (5) rivalry among existing
competitors. The core logic: the economic value an industry creates gets
apportioned -- drained by rivalry, bargained away by powerful suppliers or
buyers, or constrained by entrants and substitutes -- so the configuration of
the five forces, not just a single competitor, determines how much margin is
actually available to capture.

When Five Forces is the right lens: it is best for assessing the structural
attractiveness and competitive dynamics of a market a company operates in --
"how hard is it to make durable margin here, and why" -- rather than for
positioning one company's segments against each other (that's BCG/Three
Horizons) or auditing one company's internal factors (that's SWOT). For an
AI-first insurer, the most diagnostic forces are usually buyer power (price
transparency and easy switching in personal lines), threat of substitutes and
new entrants (low-friction digital competitors and embedded insurance), and
rivalry intensity. Its weakness: it is a snapshot of industry structure, not a
plan -- it tells you where pressure comes from, not what to do, and it
under-weights fast-moving technology disruption unless explicitly read into
the entrant/substitute forces.

Source: Michael E. Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy," Harvard Business Review, 1979 -- https://hbr.org/1979/03/how-competitive-forces-shape-strategy
