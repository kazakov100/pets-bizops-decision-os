SWOT analysis originated as the "SOFT" approach, published by the Stanford
Research Institute's (SRI) Long Range Planning Service in 1965; the cited
scholarly reconstruction of its origins names Robert Franklin Stewart as
SWOT's originator (the widely-repeated "Albert Humphrey" attribution is
contested -- Humphrey was part of the SRI group, but the archival research
credits Stewart). In the original method, each manager wrote down 8-10 key
planning issues and graded each, with evidence, as safeguarding the
_Satisfactory_, opening _Opportunities_, fixing _Faults_, or thwarting
_Threats_ -- hence SOFT, later merely relabeled to Strengths, Weaknesses,
Opportunities, Threats. The enduring discipline of the framework is the split
between the INTERNAL axis (Strengths and Weaknesses -- things the company
controls: its book of business, cost structure, capabilities) and the
EXTERNAL axis (Opportunities and Threats -- things in the market the company
does not control: competitor moves, regulation, customer sentiment shifts).

When SWOT is the right lens: it is best for a broad "whole situation in one
view" read when a business has a mix of clearly internal and clearly
external factors that a stakeholder needs to see side by side before
deciding where to act -- e.g. strong growth and brand (internal strength)
set against a structural underwriting gap (internal weakness) and
review-driven reputation risk (external threat). Its weakness as a tool: it
is descriptive, not prioritizing -- a SWOT grid lists factors but does not
itself tell you which one matters most or what to do, so it should be
paired with a follow-on prioritization step rather than treated as a
decision on its own.

Source: Albert Humphrey / Stanford Research Institute (1960-1970); see "The origins of SWOT analysis," ScienceDirect (Long Range Planning) -- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0024630123000110
