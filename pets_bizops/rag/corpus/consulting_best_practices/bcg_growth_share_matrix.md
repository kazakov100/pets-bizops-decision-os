BCG's Growth-Share Matrix (created by Bruce Henderson in 1968) classifies a
company's business units along two axes -- relative market share and market
growth rate -- into four categories. "Stars" (high growth, high share)
warrant continued heavy investment because they have high future potential.
"Cash cows" (low growth, high share) generate more cash than they need and
should be harvested to fund growth elsewhere, not over-invested in. "Question
marks" (high growth, low share) are the ambiguous case -- worth investing in
only if there's a credible path to becoming a star, otherwise a candidate to
discard. "Dogs" (low growth, low share) are candidates to divest or
reposition. The strategic logic: market leadership in a category tends to
produce durable superior returns, and most business units move through this
cycle (question mark -> star -> cash cow -> dog) as their market matures.
Applied to a multi-segment company, the useful discipline is asking which
quadrant each segment is actually in BEFORE judging it by the same yardstick
as the others -- a fast-growing, lower-share segment (question mark/star
territory) should be evaluated on share-gain trajectory, not on the cash
generation expected of a mature cash-cow segment.

Source: Boston Consulting Group, "What Is the Growth Share Matrix?" -- https://www.bcg.com/about/overview/our-history/growth-share-matrix
