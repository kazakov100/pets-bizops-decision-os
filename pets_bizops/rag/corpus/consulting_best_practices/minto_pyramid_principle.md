The Minto Pyramid Principle, developed by Barbara Minto (McKinsey's first
female MBA professional hire), structures an analysis "answer first": lead
with a single **governing thought** (the one-sentence bottom line /
recommendation), then support it with a small set of grouped supporting
arguments, each in turn backed by data. The supporting groups must be
**MECE** -- "divided pieces that were mutually exclusive of each other and
collectively exhaustive in terms of the whole" (Minto's own words). Minto's
rule: the point above must be a summary derived FROM the points below it, not
an independent assertion -- so every headline must actually follow from its
supporting evidence. As Minto puts it, "the pyramid is a tool to help you
find out what you think... it forces you to pull out of your head information
that you weren't aware was there, and then helps you to develop and shape it
until the thinking is crystal clear."

When the issue-tree / pyramid lens is the right choice: it is best when
there is a clear decision or recommendation to communicate to a busy
stakeholder, and the job is to make the logic chain from evidence ->
conclusion unmistakable. Unlike SWOT (which spreads four co-equal buckets
side by side) or a positioning matrix (which is inherently visual), the
pyramid is the right tool when you already have a point of view and need to
defend it crisply -- "here is the answer, here are the 3 reasons, here is
the data under each." Its weakness: it presumes you've already reached a
conclusion, so it's a poor fit for genuinely exploratory situations where
the answer isn't yet clear and you're still mapping the landscape.

Source: Barbara Minto, "The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking"; see McKinsey alumni profile "Barbara Minto: MECE, I invented it" -- https://www.mckinsey.com/alumni/news-and-events/global-news/alumni-news/barbara-minto-mece-i-invented-it-so-i-get-to-say-how-to-pronounce-it
